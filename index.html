🗺️ Rock cycle guide

Rocks are always slowly changing from one kind to another. Each arrow is a process. Any rock can take many paths, and the cycle has no start or finish.

🌧️ Weathering and erosion

Water, ice, wind and plants break rock into pieces called sediment: pebbles, sand, or tiny mud and clay. Some minerals dissolve into the water. Moving water, wind and ice carry the sediment away.

🏞️ Deposition

Fast water can carry big pebbles; calm water only drops the tiniest mud. So where sediment settles decides its grain size. In warm seas, living things use dissolved minerals to build shells.

🧱 Compaction and cementation

Layers pile up, get buried, squeezed and glued together into sedimentary rock. The rock’s name depends on what it is made of and the size of its grains. Check the 📖 reference chart!

⬇🔥 Heat and pressure

Deep burial changes a rock’s minerals WITHOUT melting it, making metamorphic rock. Minerals may line up in layers (foliated) or grow into locked crystals (non-foliated). Some rocks change step by step: a little heat and pressure makes one rock, and more makes another.

🌋 Melting

Very high heat melts rock into magma. Dark, iron-rich rocks make dark magma; other rocks make light-colored magma. Not every rock melts the same way: rocks made mostly of calcite break apart and give off gas instead.

❄️ Cooling

Magma that cools SLOWLY underground grows big crystals (intrusive igneous rock). Lava that cools FAST at the surface has tiny crystals or none (extrusive igneous rock). Trapped gas leaves holes.

Tip: every Rock Dex entry tells how that rock forms, and the evolution chart in the Dex remembers each change you’ve seen in the Rock Cycle Machine.

❓ How this model works

Any rock can take any path. For example, heat and pressure can change igneous rock straight into metamorphic rock.

The science rules in this model

  • Hardness (Mohs scale 1–10): a tool scratches a specimen only when the specimen is softer: scratch happens if Hspecimen < Htool. Kit: fingernail 2.5, copper penny 3.5, glass plate 5.5, steel file 6.5. If the two hardness values are within 0.25, the result is “unclear,” just like real life.
  • Streak: the color of a mineral’s powder on an unglazed porcelain plate (plate hardness about 6.5–7). Minerals harder than the plate leave no colored streak.
  • Fizz test: calcite reacts with dilute acid: CaCO₃ + 2HCl → CaCl₂ + H₂O + CO₂ (the bubbles are carbon dioxide gas).
  • Heft: specific gravity = density of the specimen ÷ density of water. Most rocks are about 2.5–3; metal ores (5–7.5) feel heavy. Anything less dense than water (under 1 g/cm³), like pumice, floats.
  • Magnetism: magnetite is the only strongly magnetic specimen in this set.
  • Rock vs. mineral: a mineral is one natural, solid, crystalline substance with a definite makeup. A rock is a mixture of minerals (or natural glass, or rock pieces).
  • Where rocks form: sedimentary rock forms near the surface in flat layers, and lower layers are usually older (law of superposition). Metamorphic rock forms deeper, changed by heat and pressure without melting. Intrusive igneous rock forms when magma cools slowly underground (big crystals). Extrusive igneous rock forms when lava cools quickly at volcanoes (tiny or no crystals).
  • Temperature: about 15 °C at the surface plus about 25 °C for every kilometer deeper (average geothermal gradient).

Rock Cycle Machine rules

  • Heat and pressure (deep burial, no melting) → metamorphic rock. Rocks change step by step, so more heat and pressure can change a metamorphic rock again. Extreme heat starts to melt it.
  • Melting, then cooling: slow cooling underground → intrusive igneous rock with large crystals; fast cooling at the surface → extrusive igneous rock with tiny or no crystals. Dark, iron-rich rocks melt into dark magma and make dark igneous rocks; all other rocks make light-colored ones.
  • Weathering, erosion, deposition, compaction and cementation → sedimentary rock. The grain size or material that settles decides which rock forms.
  • Rocks made of calcite don’t melt into ordinary magma; heat breaks calcite into lime and carbon dioxide gas instead.
  • Exact results for each rock are discovered by experimenting and recorded in the Rock Dex evolution chart.
  • XP: +40 for a correct prediction on a new path, +10 for a repeat. Challenges: +80 at par, +50 for one extra step, +30 for two (+10 for replays). Explaining a step correctly afterward: +20 bonus (+5 on replays).

Simplifications (what is not realistic)

  • Not to scale: each block stands for about 100 m of depth, but the geologist is drawn big enough to see. Real magma chambers are usually several kilometers or more down and are not under every place.
  • The layers are neat bands. Real layers are tilted, folded, faulted and partly eroded, and the order varies from place to place.
  • Each specimen shows textbook-typical properties. Real samples vary (colors change a lot, and some hematite and basalt are weakly magnetic).
  • A rock gets one overall hardness result even though its grains differ.
  • In class, the acid test uses dilute hydrochloric acid handled by the teacher with goggles, or vinegar, which fizzes more weakly.
  • ♻️ The Rock Cycle Machine shows each change in seconds, but in nature it takes thousands to millions of years. Changed rocks join your collection and Rock Dex so you can complete it, but real rocks can’t be traded this way!
  • ♻️ What melted rock becomes also depends on its chemistry. The machine uses two simple rules: dark, iron-rich basalt melts into dark rock (basalt or gabbro), and every other rock melts into light-colored magma (granite or obsidian). Light-colored lava often cools into rhyolite, a fine-grained rock not in our Rock Dex.
  • 🧱 Rebuilding puts back the rock that was originally in that spot so you can climb out or reach new places. In real life, dug-out rock can’t be put back the way it was!
  • Real rocks take thousands to millions of years to form and change. The game skips the waiting!
